Complex Numbers

“Mathematics is the Queen of Sciences and Arithmetic is the Queen of Mathematics.” – Gauss

Hi! Today we meet a number whose square is −1.

Why do we need them?

The equation \(x^2+1=0\) gives \(x^2=-1\).

The square of every real number is non-negative, so there is no real solution.

Same trouble in \(ax^2+bx+c=0\) when \(D=b^2-4ac\lt 0\).

Fix: extend the real numbers to a larger system in which \(x^2=-1\) can be solved.

y = x² + 1 never touches the x-axis

The number family

Each set sits inside the next:

\(\mathbb N\subset\mathbb Z\subset\mathbb Q\subset\mathbb R\subset\mathbb C\)

Every real number is complex: \(a=a+i0\).

\(x^2=-1\) has a solution only in \(\mathbb C\).

Complex numbers

Let \(i=\sqrt{-1}\), so \(i^2=-1\). A number \(a+ib\) with \(a,b\) real is a complex number.

e.g. \(2+i3,\ (-1)+i\sqrt3,\ 4+i\left(\dfrac{-1}{11}\right)\)

For \(z=2+i5\): \(\operatorname{Re}z=2\) (real part), \(\operatorname{Im}z=5\) (imaginary part).

Equal when both parts match:
\(a+ib=c+id\iff a=c\ \text{and}\ b=d\)
Example 1 If \(4x+i(3x-y)=3+i(-6)\), find \(x,y\).

Equate real and imaginary parts:

\(4x=3,\quad 3x-y=-6\)
\(x=\dfrac34,\quad y=\dfrac{33}{4}\)

Add and subtract

\((a+ib)+(c+id)=(a+c)+i(b+d)\)

\((2+i3)+(-6+i5)=-4+i8\)

Difference: \(z_1-z_2=z_1+(-z_2)\)

\((6+3i)-(2-i)=4+4i\)
\((2-i)-(6+3i)=-4-4i\)

Properties of addition
  • Closure: \(z_1+z_2\) is complex
  • Commutative: \(z_1+z_2=z_2+z_1\)
  • Associative: \((z_1+z_2)+z_3=z_1+(z_2+z_3)\)
  • Additive identity: \(0+i0\)
  • Additive inverse of \(z=a+ib\): \(-a+i(-b)\)

Inverse and division

For \(z=a+ib\ne0\):

\(\dfrac1z=\dfrac{a}{a^2+b^2}+i\,\dfrac{-b}{a^2+b^2}\)

Division: \(\dfrac{z_1}{z_2}=z_1\cdot\dfrac1{z_2}\), \(z_2\ne0\).

Example \(z_1=6+3i,\ z_2=2-i\)
\(\dfrac{z_1}{z_2}=(6+3i)\dfrac{2+i}{5}=\dfrac15\left[12-3+i(6+6)\right]\)
\(=\dfrac15(9+12i)\)

Powers of i

\(i^{4k}=1,\ i^{4k+1}=i,\ i^{4k+2}=-1,\ i^{4k+3}=-i\)

Example 6(ii): \(i^{-35}=\dfrac1{i^{35}}=\dfrac1{(i^2)^{17}i}=\dfrac1{-i}=i\)

Each × i is a quarter turn!

Square roots of negatives

\(i^2=-1\) and \((-i)^2=-1\), so \(-1\) has two square roots: \(i\) and \(-i\). The symbol \(\sqrt{-1}\) means \(i\) only.

\((\sqrt3\,i)^2=-3\), so \(\sqrt{-3}=\sqrt3\,i\). In general \(\sqrt{-a}=\sqrt a\,i\) for \(a\gt0\).

Trap! \(\sqrt a\sqrt b=\sqrt{ab}\) fails when both are negative:
\(i^2=\sqrt{-1}\,\sqrt{-1}=\sqrt{(-1)(-1)}=\sqrt1=1\)

That contradicts \(i^2=-1\).

Modulus and conjugate

\(|z|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\), \(\bar z=a-ib\)

\(|3+i|=\sqrt{10},\ |2-5i|=\sqrt{29},\ \overline{3+i}=3-i\)

\(z\bar z=|z|^2,\qquad z^{-1}=\dfrac{\bar z}{|z|^2}\)
Example 5 Inverse of \(2-3i\): \(|z|^2=13\), so \(z^{-1}=\dfrac{2+3i}{13}=\dfrac2{13}+\dfrac3{13}i\)
Properties
  • \(|z_1z_2|=|z_1||z_2|\)
  • \(\left|\dfrac{z_1}{z_2}\right|=\dfrac{|z_1|}{|z_2|}\)
  • \(\overline{z_1z_2}=\bar z_1\bar z_2\)
  • \(\overline{z_1\pm z_2}=\bar z_1\pm\bar z_2\)
  • \(\overline{\left(\dfrac{z_1}{z_2}\right)}=\dfrac{\bar z_1}{\bar z_2}\)

Example 8 If \(x+iy=\dfrac{a+ib}{a-ib}\), prove \(x^2+y^2=1\).
\(x+iy=\dfrac{(a+ib)^2}{a^2+b^2}=\dfrac{a^2-b^2}{a^2+b^2}+\dfrac{2ab}{a^2+b^2}i\)
\(x^2+y^2=\dfrac{(a^2-b^2)^2+4a^2b^2}{(a^2+b^2)^2}=1\)

Historical note

  • Mahavira (850): a negative quantity is not a square, so it has no square root.
  • Bhaskara (1150): says the same in his Bijaganita.
  • Cardan (1545): \(x+y=10,\ xy=40\) gave \(x=5+\sqrt{-15},\ y=5-\sqrt{-15}\). He called them “useless”.
  • Girard (about 1625): accepted them: as many roots as the degree of the equation.
  • Euler: first to use the symbol \(i\) for \(\sqrt{-1}\).
  • Hamilton (about 1830): \(a+ib\) is just the ordered pair \((a,b)\).
